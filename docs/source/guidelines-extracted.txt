Background
Global guidance is increasingly promoting integrated testing services and coordinated quality management approaches at the point-of-care (POC), particularly as countries expand access to rapid diagnostic testing across multiple diseases. Within Ministry of Health (MoH) tiered laboratory networks, diagnosis and treatment services are progressively shifting to lower levels of the health system, supported by simple, rapid tests performed at or near the patient.  Rapid diagnostic tests are widely used at many point-of-care testing sites to expand access to high-quality healthcare services and improve patient outcomes. As rapid testing grows across multiple disease programs, disease-specific tools and parallel systems can create redundancies and fragmentation. In addition, many programs do not have quality assurance systems fully in place. Global guidance increasingly emphasizes integrated testing services and coordinated quality management approaches at the POC, especially as countries expand rapid diagnostic testing for multiple diseases. An integrated approach helps maximize existing resources, reduces fragmentation, and improves long-term sustainability. However, this expansion must be accompanied by robust quality assurance systems to ensure test results remain accurate, timely, and reliable for patient management.
Strong POC testing sites require essential supporting systems and tools: up-to-date standard operating procedures for all testing processes; appropriate safety equipment and adherence to safety practices; reliable procurement and inventory management; routine quality control and external quality assessment (EQA) for all tests conducted; a trained and competent workforce; adequate on-site supervision; and effective referral pathways for confirmatory or specialized testing. Strengthening these cross-cutting elements supports quality across all rapid testing services delivered at the same site.
The Updated Checklist: Stepwise Process for Improving the Quality of Rapid Diagnostic Testing (SPI-RDT)
To support users in assessing and strengthening quality at POC testing sites, an updated Stepwise Process for Improving the Quality of Rapid Diagnostic Testing (SPI-RDT) checklist has been developed. This new checklist replaces the previous Stepwise Process for Improving the Quality of HIV Rapid and Recency Testing (SPI-RRT), which was originally designed to establish minimum quality standards and quality assurance (QA) practices for decentralized HIV rapid and recency testing sites and to support continuous quality improvement (CQI).
As the testing landscape evolves, there is an increasing need for a checklist that can be applied across multiple disease programs. Building on the success of the original checklist, the revised SPI-RDT checklist was updated to be pathogen-agnostic while retaining the proven strengths of the SPI-RRT approach, including its stepwise methodology and focus on CQI, while expanding it to enable pathogen-agnostic assessment of any rapid diagnostic testing services delivered at the same testing site. Key updates include:
Streamlining the checklist from eight sections to five to simplify use and improve efficiency.
Updating the testing section to be pathogen-agnostic, so one checklist can be used to assess multiple testing programs operating within the same site, using the same set of elements.
Clarifying that the checklist assesses the overall testing program (systems, processes, and quality practices), rather than evaluating an individual test.
Reorganizing content, so pre-test, test, and post-test elements are integrated throughout the sections as cross-cutting components of quality.
Adding an optional specimen referral section and revising point values to align scoring with the updated structure and quality expectations.
With these updates, the SPI-RDT checklist is designed to provide a unified and efficient approach to quality improvement for high-quality test results in decentralized settings across multiple disease programs. We anticipate that one site visit and a single checklist could cover all pathogens, saving time and reducing duplication. All testing programs would follow the same quality improvement framework, and assessors could be trained once to support any rapid diagnostic testing program. This consistency is expected to make it easier to identify shared gaps that affect multiple tests and allow the approach to adapt quickly as new diagnostics are introduced. Considering all testing programs together should support better planning and resource use, a strong quality framework would be in place to respond to emerging threats or new technologies.
Scope and Application
The updated checklist is intended for use at the testing-site level where POC testing is conducted. Where multiple testing sites operate within the same facility, each site is treated as an independent assessment unit and should be assessed separately.
It is important to note that the checklist is a flexible template that can be adapted by countries to meet their specific needs. Countries are encouraged to utilize the SPI-RDT tool in various facility types, including laboratories, where RDTs may also be performed. This adaptability allows the checklist to effectively address quality gaps in RDT use across different testing environments, ensuring that it remains relevant and supportive of quality improvement initiatives in both POC and laboratory settings.
By customizing the checklist, countries can enhance its applicability and maximize its potential to improve the quality of rapid diagnostic testing services delivered in their health systems
Purpose of the Users’ Guide for the SPI-RDT Checklist
The Users’ guide provides instructions for implementing the checklist in an accurate and standardized manner. It explains the intent of each question and how to assess it, including what data to collect and how to verify evidence through document review, interviews, and/or observation. Examples provided in Section IV, “Tips on How to Conduct the Assessment” are used to illustrate the methods described in that section.
Some checklist items may be outside the purview of the testing site personnel; in these cases, site supervisors and/or higher levels with MoH may need to address broader management or systems level issues. Aggregated assessment results can also support advocacy, program planning, and policy decision-making at MoH level.
The checklist, particularly the testing section, is designed to be flexible across different algorithms and pathogens. Assessors should be competent in all checklist areas, apply evidence-based scoring, and calculate total possible and achieved scores correctly.
Steps and Requirements of the Assessment
Notes:
When conducting assessments, they should be carried out as an integrated process led by the MoH team collaboratively (not in parallel or independently).
Two assessors per site visit are preferred to improve objectivity when conducting an assessment. However, if resources are limited, a single assessor may conduct the assessment.
An annual assessment plan is required to implement the ongoing evaluation of all intended sites.
Pre-Assessment Steps
(Complete these in order, refer to Appendix 1 for a pre-assessment checklist job aid)
Assessors should be previously trained and competent in the use of the checklist and in conducting assessments.
Prior to the visit, assessors should review the national testing guidelines for the pathogen testing programs included in the assessment and relevant (e.g., previous assessment reports) to determine whether previously identified gaps have been addressed.
Assessors should work with MoH to identify and prioritize sites for evaluation in alignment with the MoH annual assessment plan.
Agree on assessment dates with each site. Send a formal authorization to the facility stating the purpose of the assessment and the planned date and time. Because letters may be received late, follow up with a phone call a couple of days prior to the visit.
Print at least one hard copy of the checklist for each testing site to be assessed. Where multiple testing sites exist within a single facility, each selected testing site must have its own completed checklist.
At least one week prior to the assessment, send an electronic version of Part A (General Facility Information) of the checklist and request digital documents in advance.
Arrange transportation and, where necessary, accommodation in advance of the assessment.
Assessment
(Follow these practices during the site visit)
Introduction & Planning
Introduce the assessment team and explain the purpose. Meet the site supervisor, testers, and health team. If a courtesy visit to the Regional or District Health Management Team is required, plan for it before the site assessment. Emphasize that evaluation is supportive and developmental, not punitive.
Agree on the schedule. Confirm start and end times and build in reasonable breaks (tea and lunch) so the assessment proceeds smoothly and respects staff availability.
Review & Verification
Review past gaps and improvements. Discuss any previously identified gaps and remedial actions the site has taken. Acknowledge completed improvements and encourage continued attention to any remaining recommendations. Maintain a constructive, supportive attitude.
Verify facility/site details. Confirm the testing facility’s official name and physical address (as shown on official documents or facility entrance sign) and record dates in the country’s preferred format (see Part A).
Conducting the Assessment
Use a calm and professional approach. Conduct the assessment respectfully. If a staff member does not understand a question, rephrase it clearly. Wherever possible, assessors should communicate in the site’s native language.
Require evidence for verbal responses. Ask staff to back up verbal answers with documentation, current Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs), records, or examples of best practices.
Confirm compliance by observation when appropriate. Direct observation by the assessor (for example, the presence and use of safety equipment, or watching the tester perform procedures) is considered best practice.
Post-Assessment
(Complete these after the site visit)
Debrief with the site team. Meet at the agreed time and location with testers, supervisors, and/or management to share main findings and recommendations. Be encouraging, highlight strengths and best practices, and frame recommendations as constructive next steps.
Agree on the report timeline. Confirm the deadline for issuing the site assessment report and timeline required for remedial actions.
Follow filing procedures. File the original site assessment report in accordance with MoH procedures.
Deliver the report to the site. Ensure the site receives a copy of the report within the agreed timeframe.
Offer follow-up support. Be available to site personnel who have questions or need clarification while implementing improvements.
Invite feedback. Thank them for their time and ask any questions or suggestions to improve future assessments and the overall testing program.
Tips on How to Conduct the Assessment
General Principles
Use three sources of evidence. Evidence of compliance may be obtained through a combination of document review, observation, and staff interviews. For some requirements, direct observation alone may be sufficient (e.g., PPE use, workflow, hand hygiene). For others, documented evidence is essential (e.g., training records, QC logs, EQA reports).
Apply professional judgment. Assessors should use professional judgment while ensuring that verbal responses are supported by observable or documented evidence wherever possible. Do not accept a "Yes" statement as valid unless confirmed by actual evidence (e.g., documentation or best practices).
Ask open-ended questions. Use phrases like “show me how…” or “tell me about…” It is often unnecessary to ask all checklist questions verbatim. An experienced assessor can answer multiple checklist questions by asking open-ended questions of the testing staff.
Document Review
Verify that written procedures exist and are current. Review all available records, logs, reports, standard operating procedures (SOPs) and/or job aids) to ensure they are available and current. Observe the written SOP/job aid, as testers might be able to explain the procedure, but it is not a written SOP/job aid.
Check conformity with national guidance. Verify the correct algorithm and use of nationally approved tests (e.g., if a three-test algorithm is required for HIV diagnosis, confirm that the national algorithm is followed and only nationally approved tests are used).
Follow up on prior findings. Confirm that the testing procedures are appropriate for the pathogen being tested and must follow the national guidelines. For example, if a three-test algorithm is required for HIV diagnosis, verify that the national algorithm is followed and that only nationally approved tests are used.
Observe evidence that previous gaps were investigated, documented, and closed. If not closed, record corrective actions and add unresolved items to the report. If remediation is the responsibility of regional/national authorities, document and escalate to the designated point of contact.
Observation
Observe the full testing sequence when feasible. Wherever possible, request permission to watch one complete test (pre-test, test, post-test) to confirm that practice matches written procedures.
Scoring Guidelines
Yes (Y)= Full compliance; all elements are present and consistently implemented
Partial (P)= Some aspects are in place but not completed to full conformance or not consistently implemented.
No (N)= Lack of implementation in a particular area
Non-applicable (N/A) = The question does not apply to the testing site or the testing program. This may be an option in a few cases. No score should be recorded. Rather, N/A should prompt a short explanation in the comments section.
Special Considerations
Multiple testing sites. This checklist will be used to assess POC testing at a site(s) in a facility. Where a facility has numerous testing sites, it may not be feasible to assess each one during a single visit. It is therefore recommended to assess a subset of the testing sites (e.g., 3 or as directed by the per-national program) per visit, while ensuring that all rapid tests for all pathogens are included in the assessment.
Logical scoring sequences. Take note in Section 4: each test might give different answers to the same question. For example, question 4.22” Is a standardized logbook/register available and correctly completed?”  might be Yes for HIV, No for Malaria, and Partial for Syphilis. Do not assume each test will score the same per question.
Differentiate responsibility levels. It is important to differentiate the responsibilities of the testing sites per facility from the responsibilities of the regional or national level. Deficits in the latter are likely to result in a reduced overall score but highlight areas where the higher-level systems play a critical role in supporting and strengthening lower-level POC testing sites.

Part B. SPI-RDT Checklist
Part B constitutes the main body of the SPI-RDT Checklist for conducting the inspection and scoring. The Checklist is laid out in five sections that align with the national standard requirements.
Instructions as per guidelines
For each question, answer as follows:
Tick Yes (Y), only if full compliance is demonstrated
Tick Partial (P), if some compliance is demonstrated
Tick No (N), if there is no evidence of compliance
Tick Not Applicable (N/A), if the question is not applicable or irrelevant to the country context, a score is not required
In the Comment box, describe the specific gap (Partial and No scores) or the reason the question does not apply (N/A)
Sections 1 (Organization and Management), 2 (Physical Facility) and 3 (Safety) pertain to the overall facility incorporating the testing sites. Section 4 (Testing) assesses the testing site(s).

Part C. Scoring
This section outlines the scoring criteria and explains how to calculate actual scores and final percentages based on the total possible score. This section is completed post-assessment visit.
YES indicates ALL elements are present. Evidence of compliance must be available through observation or the review of documents (e.g., SOPs, job aids, records, logs, etc.).
PARTIAL indicates some implementation of compliance but not full or inconsistent compliance, e.g. Job descriptions are available for some but not all testers.
NO indicates no evidence of compliance, e.g., no training records of staff performing testing, staff do not follow the SOP
Non-applicable (N/A) means a question is not relevant to the POC testing site.  Be aware of such answers as they need to be eliminated from the total possible score.
YES is awarded 2 points, PARTIAL is awarded 1 point and NO is awarded zero points
Note: each section’s total possible score needs to be calculated by the assessor:
If an answer is N/A, that question must be excluded from the total possible score, e.g. if a section is worth 20 points (10 questions) and 9 questions score YES (2 points each), but one question is N/A, then the total for that section is 18 out of 18

Within Section B4 Testing, the total possible scores might vary significantly across the tests according to the quality measures implemented per test.
After completing section B, calculate all possible scores and all actual scores obtained
